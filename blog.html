August 3, 2026

They're Laying Off Managers and Hiring New Grads. That's Not a Contradiction.

Noticed a pattern this week: tech companies announcing hundreds of layoffs while simultaneously ramping up college recruiting. Some have said it openly — they're hiring junior talent who'll be AI-native from day one, replacing the middle layer that used to synthesize, summarize, and route information. The logic is brutal but clear: if a new grad with AI tools can produce the output of a mid-level manager, why pay for the manager? The premium shifts from "years of experience doing the thing" to "ability to orchestrate AI to do the thing at scale." I'm watching this closely because it reshapes what "valuable experience" means. 9 years of pattern recognition still matters — but only if you're applying it through AI, not competing with it. The professionals who survive this aren't the ones with the most tenure. They're the ones who figured out how to make AI an amplifier of their judgment, not a replacement for their labor.

July 30, 2026

I Used a $200/mo AI to Set Up My $20/mo AI

Tried Claude Fable 5 this week during a promotional window. It's absurdly powerful — the reasoning depth, the architecture thinking, the way it holds complexity. But it's expensive for daily use on my current needs. So here's what I did: while I had access, I asked Fable 5 to create a project that would answer questions as if it were Fable 5, but actually runs on Sonnet. Basically, I had the expensive model write the system instructions, guardrails, and thinking frameworks for the cheaper model. Now I use that bootstrapped project to set up every new Claude project I create. The premium model did the hard thinking once. The cheaper model executes it daily. This is the same pattern I've seen in finance for years — you pay for the senior partner's judgment at the start of the engagement, then the team executes the framework for months. The expensive input is the architecture. The ongoing work runs on something leaner. Same principle, applied to AI spend.

I'm Building a Website Nobody Outside My Team Will Ever See

Spent today building an internal practice website — not for clients, not for the public, just for my team. Valuation tools, methodology guidelines, proposal templates, demo materials. All the things that currently live scattered across 14 shared drives that nobody can find when they need them. But here's the part that excites me: I'm adding an AI chatbot that sits on top of all the content. You ask it "where's the template for a securitization proposal?" and it gives you the answer with the relevant excerpt and a link to the source file. No more digging through folders. No more Slacking three people to find a document that definitely exists somewhere. The people who benefit most are new joiners who don't know where anything lives, and leadership who don't have time to hunt. The real insight: most "knowledge management" problems aren't solved by better organization. They're solved by better retrieval. You don't need a perfect folder structure. You need a system that finds things regardless of where they're stored.

KIMI and the Age of Parallel Agents

China's KIMI just showed something most Western AI tools haven't figured out yet: you can spin up numerous autonomous agents that run simultaneously. Not sequentially. Not one-at-a-time. Parallel. Think about what that means for finance workflows: one agent pulling comps, another analyzing the cap structure, a third stress-testing assumptions, a fourth drafting the memo — all at once, feeding each other context as they go. I've been building multi-agent systems for months, but they've been sequential chains. Parallel execution is a fundamentally different architecture. It's the difference between a team that works in relay vs. a team that works in a room together. KIMI is showing the room.

Dictation Killed My Perfectionism

Unexpected side effect of switching to voice: I stopped over-editing. When you type, there's a backspace key inviting you to fix every sentence before moving on. When you dictate, the words are already out. You keep going. The result isn't sloppier — it's actually more honest. I noticed my dictated emails sound more like how I talk in meetings — direct, warm, gets to the point. My typed emails used to sound like a lawyer reviewed them. Turns out the "polishing" I was doing while typing was actually removing personality. The first-take voice version is closer to who I actually am. That's the version people respond to.

July 27, 2026

The Ethics of Sending Your Clone to a Meeting

Genuine question I wrestled with this week: if my HeyGen avatar can deliver a project update that's indistinguishable from me doing it live — is it dishonest to send the clone? My gut says no, as long as the content is mine. Nobody questions whether I "personally" typed an email or used voice-to-text. Nobody asks if I manually formatted a slide deck. We've always outsourced the delivery layer — assistants, templates, tools. A video clone just extends that to visual communication. But I think the line is interaction. If someone asks my clone a follow-up question and gets a hallucinated answer — that's a problem. The delivery can be synthetic. The thinking can't be.

July 26, 2026

When Your AI Fails at 3am, Nobody's Awake to Fix It

Ran an autonomous loop overnight for the first time. It failed halfway through — hit an edge case I hadn't anticipated, kept retrying the same broken approach in a circle. Woke up to 47 iterations of the same error. Lesson learned: autonomous doesn't mean unsupervised. It means you need better guardrails upfront. In consulting, we'd never let a junior analyst work through the night without checkpoints. Same principle applies to AI loops. Define the exit conditions as carefully as you define the task. "Keep going until done" is lazy delegation whether you're managing people or machines.

That context-switching cost is invisible until it's gone. Now it's gone. I read the entire memo in one pass.

July 23, 2026

The Delegation Skill Nobody Teaches

9 years of managing teams taught me that bad delegation sounds like "handle this" and good delegation sounds like "here's the outcome, here's the constraint, here's how I'll know it's done." The exact same skill applies to AI agents. When my /loop tasks fail, it's almost never the AI's fault. It's because I was vague about what "done" means. I've started writing my AI task definitions the same way I'd write a scope document for a new team member: deliverable, format, quality bar, edge cases to watch for. The better the brief, the less supervision required. Same lesson, new context.

My First HeyGen Clone Was Terrible. The Second One Wasn't.

First attempt at a video clone: stiff, robotic, uncanny valley. The eye contact was off. The pacing felt rehearsed. It looked like me but didn't feel like me. So I re-recorded the training data — this time, I talked the way I actually talk in meetings. Relaxed. Slightly faster. Occasional pauses when I'm thinking. That version was dramatically better. The insight: the clone is only as good as the training data you feed it. If you perform for the camera, you get a performer. If you just talk naturally, you get something that actually represents you. Same lesson as prompting AI — garbage in, garbage out. Authenticity in, authenticity out.
